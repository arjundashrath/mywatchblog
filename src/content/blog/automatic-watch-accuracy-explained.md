You spend ₹30,000 on a beautiful automatic watch, admire the dial, appreciate the craftsmanship, and wear it proudly for a few days. Then you notice something strange. Your watch is running almost a minute ahead of your phone. You correct the time, only to find it running ahead again a few days later. Meanwhile, that ₹2,000 Casio you've had for years is still keeping time perfectly well.

Naturally, the first question is whether something is wrong with your expensive new watch. The surprising answer is that there probably isn't. Mechanical watches, including automatics, are inherently less accurate than quartz watches. Even relatively expensive ones can gain or lose several seconds every day, and that's often completely normal.

But how much inaccuracy is acceptable? Why does it happen, and when should you actually be concerned? Let's get into it.

## First, Why Are Automatic Watches Less Accurate Than Quartz?

The answer lies in how they keep time. A quartz watch uses a battery-powered electronic oscillator, typically a quartz crystal vibrating at a very stable frequency. A mechanical watch, on the other hand, relies on a mainspring, a system of gears, and a balance wheel oscillating back and forth.

An automatic watch is simply a mechanical watch that winds its mainspring using the movement of your wrist. There's no electronic oscillator keeping everything in check. Instead, dozens of tiny components must work together with remarkable consistency to measure the passage of time.

The balance wheel and hairspring form the heart of this system, regulating how quickly energy moves through the mechanism. Even small variations in their behaviour can affect how accurately the watch runs. Gravity, temperature, winding conditions, and physical shocks can all influence the result.

Quartz watches have their own limitations, of course, but their electronic timekeeping systems are far less sensitive to many of these everyday variables. That's why an inexpensive quartz watch can easily outperform a mechanical watch costing ten or twenty times as much when it comes to pure accuracy.

Does that make automatic watches inferior? Not necessarily. It simply means you're buying something different. A mechanical watch is a miniature machine that measures time through springs, gears, and carefully controlled motion. The engineering is part of the appeal, even if your phone is better at telling you whether it's exactly 3:42 PM.

## What Does +15 or -20 Seconds Per Day Actually Mean?

When manufacturers discuss mechanical watch accuracy, they usually express it as a daily rate, measured in seconds per day.

A watch rated at +15 seconds per day runs approximately 15 seconds fast over 24 hours. A watch running at -20 seconds per day loses approximately 20 seconds over the same period.

If your watch consistently gains 15 seconds every day, it could be almost two minutes ahead after a week. That might sound like a lot, especially when we're accustomed to electronic devices that keep nearly perfect time.

Here's what different daily rates would mean if they remained constant.

| Daily rate | Difference after 7 days |
| --- | --- |
| +5 seconds | 35 seconds fast |
| +10 seconds | 1 minute 10 seconds fast |
| +20 seconds | 2 minutes 20 seconds fast |
| -15 seconds | 1 minute 45 seconds slow |
| -30 seconds | 3 minutes 30 seconds slow |

These are mathematical examples, not measurements of any particular watch.

There's another important distinction here: **accuracy and consistency aren't quite the same thing.** A watch that gains 10 seconds every single day is behaving consistently, even though it's running fast. Another watch might gain 20 seconds one day, lose 10 the next, and run almost perfectly on the third.

Both might average out to similar accuracy over a longer period, but their day-to-day behaviour is very different. That's why manufacturers such as Seiko recommend assessing mechanical accuracy over approximately a week rather than judging a watch from one day's performance.

## Why Do Automatic Watches Gain or Lose Time?

Several factors influence mechanical accuracy, and most of them are perfectly ordinary parts of owning an automatic watch.

### 1. Gravity and the Position of Your Watch

Gravity affects the balance wheel and other components inside a mechanical movement. Depending on the watch's orientation, those components can behave slightly differently, resulting in changes to its timekeeping rate.

For example, a watch lying flat with its dial facing upwards might run at a different rate from the same watch resting on its side with the crown pointing upwards. This is known as positional variation, and it's one reason watchmakers test movements in different positions.

It also explains why your watch might gain time while you're wearing it but lose a little time overnight when it's resting on your bedside table. Some owners experiment with different overnight positions to offset the time their watches gain or lose during the day.

There's no universal resting position that makes every watch more accurate, though. It depends on the individual movement and how it has been adjusted.

### 2. How Much the Mainspring Is Wound

An automatic watch gets its energy from a coiled mainspring. As the mainspring unwinds, the amount of force it delivers changes, and that can affect the movement's accuracy.

Modern mechanical movements are designed to regulate this energy as consistently as possible, but they cannot eliminate every variation. A watch operating with a well-wound mainspring may behave differently from one that's close to exhausting its power reserve.

This matters particularly if you rotate between several watches. If you pick up an automatic that has been sitting unused for a day or two, its mainspring may be partially wound or completely exhausted.

For watches that support manual winding, following the manufacturer's winding instructions before wearing them can help establish more consistent operating conditions. Regular wrist movement will then help keep an automatic movement wound.

### 3. Temperature Changes

Temperature can also affect mechanical accuracy because the metal components inside a watch expand and contract as temperatures change. Modern movements use materials and designs intended to minimise these effects, but they aren't entirely immune.

For owners in India, this is worth remembering. A watch worn outdoors on a hot afternoon in Delhi or Hyderabad may experience different conditions from one spending most of its day in an air-conditioned office.

That doesn't mean your watch will suddenly become wildly inaccurate whenever you step outside. Ordinary temperature variations are something watches are designed to handle. However, extreme temperatures and prolonged exposure to heat are best avoided, both for accuracy and the watch's overall condition.

### 4. Magnetism

Magnetism is one of the more interesting causes of mechanical watch inaccuracy because it can sometimes produce a sudden and noticeable change in timekeeping.

Strong magnetic fields can affect certain components inside a movement, particularly the hairspring. If the hairspring becomes magnetised, its behaviour can change, potentially causing the watch to run significantly faster or otherwise become inaccurate.

Everyday objects such as magnetic phone accessories, speakers, and magnetic clasps can contain magnets, although the risk depends on the strength of the magnetic field, distance, exposure, and the watch's resistance to magnetism.

There's no need to panic every time your watch gets near a laptop. Just avoid deliberately placing it against strong magnets for extended periods.

If your watch suddenly starts gaining several minutes per day despite previously running normally, magnetisation is one possibility worth investigating. It isn't the only possible explanation, so a watchmaker should confirm the cause before any corrective work.

### 5. Physical Shocks and Wear

Mechanical movements contain extremely small and precisely fitted components. Although modern watches often include shock-protection systems, a hard impact can still affect their performance.

Dropping a watch onto a tiled floor, for example, could potentially disturb its regulation or damage internal components. Strong, repeated impacts may also affect timekeeping.

Normal daily activities are generally fine for a watch designed for everyday use, but it's worth considering whether a sudden accuracy problem began after a fall or unusually hard knock.

Over time, lubrication can also deteriorate and components can wear. A watch that gradually becomes less consistent over several years might benefit from professional servicing rather than simply having its time adjusted.

## How Accurate Should Your Automatic Watch Be?

This is where things become interesting, because there's no single accuracy standard that applies to every automatic watch.

Different movements have different manufacturer specifications, and a higher price doesn't automatically guarantee a tighter tolerance.

Consider Seiko's popular 4R35 and 4R36 movements, used in numerous affordable mechanical watches. Seiko specifies an accuracy range of **+45 to -35 seconds per day** under its stated conditions. That means a watch gaining 30 seconds per day could still fall within the manufacturer's published tolerance.

For someone accustomed to quartz accuracy, that can sound rather disappointing. However, it's important to understand that these are manufacturer tolerances, not predictions of how every individual watch will perform. Some examples may run considerably more accurately in everyday use.

At the other end of the spectrum, there's chronometer certification.

A traditional COSC-certified mechanical chronometer must meet an average daily rate of -4 to +6 seconds per day under controlled testing conditions. COSC tests movements across different positions and temperatures, examining not only their average accuracy but also their consistency.

| Movement or standard | Published daily accuracy |
| --- | --- |
| Seiko 4R35 / 4R36 | -35 to +45 seconds |
| COSC Certified Chronometer | -4 to +6 seconds |
| COSC Excellence Chronometer | -2 to +4 seconds |

*These are different manufacturer specifications and certification benchmarks, not directly comparable guarantees of everyday wrist accuracy. COSC's traditional certification generally tests movements, while its newer Excellence certification evaluates finished watches under an additional protocol.*

A chronometer-certified watch is expected to meet a much more demanding standard than an ordinary mechanical movement. However, certification doesn't mean the watch will never deviate from those numbers in actual use.

The important takeaway is to check the specification for your particular movement before deciding whether its performance is acceptable.

## How to Check Your Automatic Watch's Accuracy at Home

You don't need professional equipment to get a reasonable idea of how accurately your watch is running. A smartphone, a reliable time reference, and a little patience are enough for a basic assessment.

Start by ensuring the watch is sufficiently wound according to its manufacturer's instructions. Synchronise its seconds hand against a reliable reference clock, such as a properly synchronised phone or an online reference showing accurate time.

Wear the watch normally and compare it against the same reference at approximately the same time each day. Record how many seconds ahead or behind it is. Try to maintain reasonably consistent wearing habits, and note anything unusual, such as leaving the watch unworn for an entire day.

A simple tracking table is sufficient.

| Day | Difference from reference | Change since previous day |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Start | 0 seconds | — |
| Day 1 | +8 seconds | +8 seconds |
| Day 2 | +19 seconds | +11 seconds |
| Day 3 | +24 seconds | +5 seconds |
| Day 4 | +34 seconds | +10 seconds |
| Day 5 | +41 seconds | +7 seconds |
| Day 6 | +53 seconds | +12 seconds |
| Day 7 | +60 seconds | +7 seconds |

*Illustrative data only, not results from a tested watch.*

In this hypothetical example, the watch has gained 60 seconds over seven days, giving an average daily rate of approximately +8.6 seconds.

Notice that the watch doesn't gain exactly the same amount every day. That's normal behaviour for a mechanical movement, and it's precisely why checking over several days provides a more useful picture than taking one measurement.

The daily change is calculated by subtracting the previous day's recorded offset from the current day's offset. A positive number indicates a gain; a negative number indicates a loss. For meaningful results, don't reset the watch during the test.

There are also smartphone apps designed to help measure mechanical watch accuracy. Some compare the watch against a reference clock, while others attempt to estimate the movement's rate using sound. These can be useful, but their reliability depends on the method and recording conditions.

A professional timegrapher is different. It listens to the movement's ticking and estimates parameters such as rate, amplitude, and beat error. It's useful for diagnosing and regulating a movement, but its instantaneous readings in a particular position aren't necessarily identical to the average accuracy you'll experience while wearing the watch.

## Can You Make Your Automatic Watch More Accurate?

There are a few things you can do to encourage more consistent timekeeping, although none will magically turn an ordinary automatic into a chronometer.

The simplest is to keep the watch adequately wound. Wearing an automatic regularly helps maintain its power reserve, and manual winding can be useful where the movement supports it. Always follow the specific manufacturer's instructions, especially if you're unsure whether your watch can be wound by hand.

You can also experiment with its overnight resting position. If your watch consistently gains time while worn, you might find that leaving it in a particular position overnight reduces the total gain. This is individual to each watch, so don't assume that placing every watch crown-up will have the same effect.

Avoid exposing your watch to strong magnetic fields or extreme temperatures, and protect it from unnecessary shocks. These are sensible precautions regardless of whether you're actively trying to improve accuracy.

If the watch consistently runs outside its expected tolerance, professional regulation may help. Regulation involves adjusting the movement to change its rate, ideally after checking its behaviour in multiple positions.

However, regulation has limits. It cannot necessarily correct underlying wear, damaged components, poor lubrication, or excessive positional variation. Sometimes a proper service is needed.

And please don't start opening the caseback and adjusting the regulator yourself after watching a five-minute tutorial. Mechanical movements are delicate, and a small mistake can become a rather expensive lesson.

## When Should You Actually Be Worried?

A watch gaining or losing a few seconds every day isn't automatically a problem. Even deviations of several tens of seconds may fall within the published tolerance of some entry-level mechanical movements.

What matters is the movement's specification, its usual behaviour, and whether anything has changed unexpectedly.

| What you're noticing | What it could mean |
| --- | --- |
| A steady gain of 10–15 seconds per day | May be normal, depending on the movement |
| Different rates on different days | Normal positional or winding variation, within reasonable limits |
| Suddenly gaining several minutes per day | Possible magnetisation or another movement problem |
| Watch stops overnight | Insufficient winding, exhausted power reserve, or a mechanical issue |
| Accuracy worsens after a hard impact | Possible disturbance or damage to the movement |
| Gradual deterioration over several years | Potential need for inspection or servicing |

These are possibilities, not diagnoses. A watchmaker can examine the movement and determine what's actually happening.

If your watch is new and consistently outside its published tolerance, contact the seller or authorised service centre before paying for independent repairs. Warranty coverage varies by manufacturer and by the nature of the problem.

Likewise, if a previously reliable watch suddenly begins running several minutes fast or slow every day, don't simply dismiss it as mechanical character. A sudden change deserves investigation, particularly if it follows an impact or suspected magnetic exposure.

Servicing intervals also vary by movement, manufacturer, and usage. Follow the recommendations for your particular watch rather than assuming every automatic needs a complete service at the same fixed interval.

## So, Are Automatic Watches Worth It If They're Less Accurate?

If accuracy is your single biggest priority, a quartz watch is almost certainly the more sensible choice. It's generally more accurate, requires less attention, and can be considerably cheaper. There's no point pretending otherwise.

But mechanical watches have never been interesting purely because they tell the time better than everything else. Their appeal lies in the engineering, the craftsmanship, and the fact that a tiny collection of springs and gears can keep track of time without relying on a battery-powered electronic oscillator.

That doesn't mean we should romanticise poor performance. A mechanical watch should still work properly, and owners have every reason to expect it to perform within reasonable limits for its movement.

The next time your automatic watch gains 20 seconds, don't immediately assume something has gone wrong. Check its specifications, observe its behaviour over several days, and see whether the deviation is consistent.

You might discover that your watch is performing perfectly normally. And if it isn't, at least you'll have a better idea of what to investigate.

**A mechanical watch doesn't have to be perfectly accurate to be a good watch. But understanding its limitations makes owning one considerably more enjoyable.**
